# Voice parsing — the rules and the transcripts behind them

Every rule below was paid for by a real mis-transcription during device testing on 2026-08-14, not by a hypothetical. `CLAUDE.md` carries the one-line rules; this file keeps the worked example that explains each one, so a rule that looks arbitrary can be checked against the sentence that caused it.

`src/utils/voiceParse.ts` is deliberately low-precision — substring/prefix matching against the user's own chip lists, not NLP:

- Needle words under 4 characters need an exact word match; longer ones need a 4-character shared prefix. An earlier 5-char-stem rule let filler words ("a", "me", "my") match unrelated entries — a test sentence produced "Aura", "Alcohol" and "Menstruation" from a transcript containing none of them.
- **A severity window stops where the medication clause starts.** Numbers after "took"/"had" are quantities, not severities: "in the back of my head ten. I took two tablets…" came back with "ten" transcribed as "then", and the occiput helped itself to the two out of "two tablets" and displayed it as a severity of 2.
- **A dose's bare hour is disambiguated by the attack, not guessed.** "A tablet of Sumatriptan at six" is ambiguous alone — which is why the *start* time refuses to resolve a bare hour at all — but a dose has to fall between the attack starting and now, and that usually leaves exactly one candidate (six o'clock, for an attack that started at noon, can only be the evening). Where it doesn't leave exactly one, the dose keeps "no time given" rather than picking a side. This is why `startMinutesAgo` is parsed *before* the doses in `parseVoiceEntry`.
- **A number word between the form and the drug name is skipped.** "Two tablets of Treo" came back as "two tablets of three trail"; taking "three" as the name meant the dose was thrown away entirely, since a number word can't be a medication.
- **A number stated for one area is never borrowed by another.** If any mention carries its own number, the sentence-wide fallback is switched off and an area without one is reported as not heard. "Joe six left eye for left forehead nine" (spoken: *jaw six, left eye four*) otherwise handed the jaw's six to the eye, and an invented severity reads exactly like a spoken one. The fallback still applies when *no* mention has its own number, which is the "eight out of ten, right eye and forehead" case.
- **Mis-transcribed area names are matched by sound** (`soundex`), because dictation mangles them past any spelling comparison — "jaw" came back as "Joe". Safe only because the candidate list is 8 fixed words. Guarded twice: the match must be a whole word of 3+ characters, **and** a number must follow within 15 characters, since this exists to rescue the "area N" dictation shape. Without that guard "nap" matches Nape and "check" matches Cheek — same soundex code — but an ordinary word that rhymes with a body part is very unlikely to be followed by a severity.
- **Start times accept a clock time as well as a relative one** — "at 7:30 am", "7.30", "at 8pm", and spoken forms like "last night around nine" or "this morning at seven" — resolved against today and rolled back a day if the result would be in the future (or if "last night"/"yesterday" was said). Checked after the relative phrases so it can't steal the number out of "two hours ago". A spoken hour **requires** a part of day: a bare "nine" could be either end of the day, and guessing puts the attack twelve hours out. An answer that can't be pinned down is shown verbatim on the review screen with a note, never rendered as "Just now".
- **Everyday phrasings map to the anatomical zone names** (`AREA_SYNONYMS`) — "back of my head" → Occiput, "top of my head" → Crown, "neck" → Nape, "brow" → Forehead, "sinuses" → Nose. Nobody says "occiput"; the zone names have to stay stable for stored data, so the vocabulary gap is bridged here. Matched before the anatomical terms so a phrase is claimed as a whole.
- **"top of" and "back of" take whatever word comes next, not literally "head".** Dictation turned "top of my head" into "top of my Hedegård" — a name, not a near-miss "head" sounds like — and the whole phrase, severity included, was silently dropped. In a migraine transcript "top of my ___" only ever means the crown, so any trailing word counts; "back of my ___" is genuinely ambiguous with the neck, so it excludes only a literal, correctly-heard "neck" (the dedicated "back of my neck" pattern still claims that case) and treats everything else as the head. The exclusion is a plain string check on the captured word, not a regex lookahead in front of the optional "my "/"the " — a lookahead there can be satisfied by backtracking past "my" itself rather than past the real word, quietly matching the wrong token.
- **"write" and "rite" count as "right"** for side detection. Dictation renders it that way often enough to matter — it turned "right jaw" into "Write jaw", which read as no side at all and selected both.
- **Every occurrence of an area term is its own reading, and each "left"/"right" belongs to the mention it is nearest.** Matching only the first occurrence collapsed "left eye nine right eye seven" into a single mention that saw both side words and gave both eyes the same severity. Assigning side words to their nearest mention (rather than each mention scanning a window around itself) is what stops the "right" in "right eye seven forehead three" reaching the forehead as well and silently making an unsided area one-sided.
- **"Nearest" is biased forward, because English puts the side word before the area.** A mention that *follows* the side word beats one that precedes it, even when the preceding one is closer in raw characters (`BACKWARD_PENALTY`, ≥ `SIDE_RANGE`, so any in-range forward candidate wins). "Right eye eight left side of the jaw seven" otherwise gave the "left" to the *eye* — 7 characters back, past "eight" — rather than the jaw, 13 forward past "side of the". The eye then held both "right" and "left", which reads as no side at all and selects both eyes, while the jaw was left unsided and selected both jaws: two clearly-sided areas became four, and the two invented ones carried severities the user never said for them.
- A side-less mention ("my forehead is killing me") selects **both** sides rather than guessing.
- **Every dose is its own reading.** People describe medication as a sequence — "two tablets last night around six and then two more this morning at eleven" — and that's two events, not one attribute of the attack. `extractDoses` returns each dose in spoken order with the time read from the words between it and the next dose, and `LogForm` turns every dose that carries a time into its own snapshot (same pain areas, since a snapshot is the full state at that moment; only the medication is new). A dose with **no** time stays on the initial reading, and a dose earlier than the stated start is clamped to the start. The first dose must not appear in both places — that would record the same tablets twice, which is why the initial reading only takes an untimed dose. These extra readings are passed into `startAttack` rather than added afterwards, because `addSnapshots` maps over the `attacks` of the current render and cannot see an attack created in the same tick.
- **Medications match against `recentMeds` first, then against phrasing.** History is the strongest signal — people take the same few things — and it brings the usual dose with it. But history alone can never catch a drug named for the first time, which is exactly what happened with "two tablets of Treo" on a fresh install. So `extractMedicationByPhrasing` also reads "*N* tablets of *X*" (name + dose), "took *X*" (name), and "took *N* tablets" (dose only, which still saves typing). A dose said out loud beats the remembered one, since it describes *this* time. `NOT_A_MED` keeps "took a shower" and "took two tablets" from naming a drug — number words are in that set for the second case, and time words ("noon", "morning", "last") because the connector before a name can just as easily introduce a time.
- **A dose's name is optional; its quantity and form are what make it a dose.** Dictation dropped the drug name out of "one tablet of Treo this morning at seven" and left "one tablet of **this** morning at seven" — "this" is a `NOT_A_MED` word, and rejecting the name used to discard the entire dose, its time included. A quantity and a form alone are still unambiguously a dose, so it survives with an empty name and its own timestamp ("1 tablet at 07:00"), which `LogForm` shows as *Unnamed* and the user can correct. Silently dropping a medication event out of a health record is the worse failure. `at` also joins a form to a name as often as `of` does ("two tablets **at** Sumatriptan"), and with only `of` allowed that dose didn't match at all — so it lost its time and collapsed into an attribute of the attack instead of an event on the timeline.
- **A rejected name must not swallow the time that follows it.** `DOSE_PATTERN` carries the `d` flag so `extractDoses` can restart the time window at the end of the *form* group when the captured name was rejected. Otherwise "took two tablets at noon" matches "noon" as the name, consumes it, and leaves an empty window — a dose with no time, when "noon" *was* the time.
- **A dose whose name dictation dropped doesn't outrank one that kept it.** The wizard's single medication field holds one dose, so `extractMedicationByPhrasing` takes the first *named* dose rather than the first dose outright.
- The raw transcript is always kept verbatim as the note, so nothing said is lost when the structured parse misses, and the prefill banner lists exactly what was recognised so a wrong guess is visible rather than silently saved.
- **Numbers count whether spoken as digits or words.** Siri transcribes "eight" as often as "8", and which one you get is invisible to the user. The general fallback matched digits only, so `"right eye eight and forehead seven"` — a sentence with two clearly stated severities — found none and defaulted both to 5. Spelled-out numbers were only recognised directly after "severity"/"pain"/"level".
- **Severity is per area, not per sentence.** `snapshot.areas` maps each zone to its own number and people speak that way: each mentioned area takes the first number said *after* it and before the next area is mentioned. A number said before any area ("eight out of ten, right eye and forehead") falls outside every forward window, so the sentence-wide severity covers it and all areas share it — which is what was meant. Side detection still uses its own ±15-character window, so `"right eye and forehead"` with no numbers between them can read "right" as belonging to the forehead too; visible in the banner, and the picker makes it trivial to correct.
- **A trailing severity is shared the same way a leading one is.** "Forehead and eye hurt, it's a nine" landed the 9 in the *last* mention's own window purely because that's where the sentence ends — not because the user aimed it at that area specifically — which used to switch the shared fallback off for forehead and invent a default for it while reporting it as not heard, despite one severity plainly covering both. The "never borrow one area's number for another" guard (`Joe six left eye for left forehead nine`) only earns its keep when mentions carry **different** numbers; a single number occupying one window is the leading-fallback case mirrored, not evidence of per-area intent, so it's shared across every mention with no number of its own.
- **A severity that wasn't heard is never printed like one that was.** Siri ends dictation on its own and routinely cuts people off before the severity, since it tends to come last. `DEFAULT_SEVERITY` (5) fills the gap because `areas` needs a number, but `severityHeard` records that it was invented and the banner says *"no severity heard, set to 5"* instead of the usual `Eye right (5)` — which reads exactly like something the user said. The prompt asks "Where does it hurt, and how bad out of ten?" for the same reason: a narrow question fits inside the window Siri allows, where an open-ended one ran past it.
- **`severityHeard` is all-areas; `severityHeardFor` is per-area — the review screen needs the second.** They disagree constantly, because a transcript that rates three areas and not the fourth is the ordinary case, not an edge one. `LogForm`'s review rows drove off the single flag, so one unrated area labelled **every** row "no severity heard" — with the verbatim transcript quoted directly underneath showing the numbers the user had plainly said. Telling someone their own spoken severity wasn't heard is the same class of lie as printing an invented one as fact, just pointed the other way. The one-tap save still gates on the all-areas flag: any invented number in the record has to be looked at.
- **The day and the half of the day are read once, for every clock branch.** They used to be read only by the spoken-hour branch, so a *numeric* hour ignored both: "Yesterday at 10:30" recorded this morning, and "last night at 10:30" recorded 10:30 today rather than 22:30 yesterday — a dose landing twelve hours out, or a start time landing a whole day out, with nothing on screen to suggest a guess had been made. An explicit `am`/`pm` suffix still wins over an inferred part of day.
- **A word another area already claimed literally is never sound-matched.** `neck` and `nose` share the soundex code N200, so "the back of the neck three" produced the correct Nape reading *and* an invented Nose one at the same severity — the trailing number satisfies the "a number must follow" guard, which exists for "Joe six" and cannot tell the two apart. A word inside an existing mention's span was transcribed correctly and needs no rescue; the synonyms run first, so the span is already there to check against.
- **The synonym exclusion strips trailing punctuation.** `(\S+)` captures "neck." at the end of a sentence, which isn't equal to "neck", so "the back of the neck." slipped past the `back of my ___` exclusion and took an Occiput reading on top of its Nape one.
